Though the superior fineness of the Peruvian Standing Liberty Eight Reales compared to commonly accepted standards may
have been interpreted as a testament to its quality by the mintmasters at Lima, in practice excess weight or superior fineness
often led to such pieces being withdrawn from circulation for their surplus intrinsic value. Nevertheless, this type is among the
most common chopmarked crowns among its contemporaries struck during the second quarter of the 19th century, and can
frequently be found as a host for counterstamps of the Philippines issued under the authority of Ferdinand VII and Isabel II
(see p. 162-165). At approximately the same time as the introduction of the type, foreign merchants were probing the Canton
System for weaknesses, attempting to break the monopoly of the hong merchants and deal with a greater range of ‘shopmen’
(per The Canton Register Vol. 1, No. 30, August 2, 1828), which might have introduced a greater range of types into circulation.
The mark shown on the coin depicted in Fig. 171 is of a distinctive type; the size is unusual for the period (larger
chopmarks were typically being applied in the mid-19th century), and hosts bearing this mark are not often recorded with other
chops. The mark is explored in greater detail on p. 233.
