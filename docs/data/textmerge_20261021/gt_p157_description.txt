Though they became independent entities upon their liberation from Spain, both Peru and Bolivia maintained popular
movements for reunification. In 1827, Peruvian forces invaded Bolivia with the intent to create a shared government, entering
La Paz in 1828 and forcing the resignation of Antonio José de Sucre, a close ally of Simón Bolívar, who maintained his own
vision of South American unity under his nation of Gran Colombia. Subsequent years were chaotic, including the declaration
of war by Gran Colombia on Peru, which came to an end with the deaths of both Sucre and Bolívar in 1830. A unification
plan was not put in place until 1836, with the emergence of the Peru–Bolivian Confederation, composed of three states:
Bolivia, North Peru, and South Peru; the confederation was motivated by both a shared history and ethnic similarities, but also
by plans for a reemergence of the commercial prominence that the region had previously enjoyed when united under Spanish
authority. However, the state was loosely arranged and short-lived. Argentina and Chile, fearful of the influence that a unified
state of such magnitude could wield, quickly entered into an alliance to confront and dissolve the combined state in the War
of the Confederation, which concluded with the Battle of Yungay in 1839, after which both Peru and Bolivia were restored as
independent nations.
During the Confederation, the coinage of the state was altered appropriately. North Peru, which included Lima and
its coinage facilities, largely retained the Standing Liberty design, with the alteration of the legend to reflect the new nomencla-
ture, which exists as both ‘EST NOR-PERUANO’ and ‘REP NOR-PERUANA’. Both are considerably scarcer than the stan-
dard Republic Standing Liberty Eight Reales, possibly due in part to the debasement of silver coinage (moneda feble) undertaken
by the Peruvian government from approximately 1835-45.
